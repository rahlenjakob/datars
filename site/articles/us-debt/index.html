Federal budget

$40 trillion, to the penny

The Treasury counts what the United States owes every business day, down to the cent. The figure below is the latest it has published: this page asks for it each time you open it.

By the datars graphics deskSept. 28, 2026

Every business day, the Treasury's Bureau of the Fiscal Service publishes a single number: everything the federal government owed at the close of the day before, to the cent. On Sept. 24, 2026, that was $40,068,807,991,924.84.

It does not stand still for long. Over the 12 months to that day the total grew by $2.6 trillion, about $7.2 billion a day, or roughly $83,000 a second. The chart at the top of this page is not a copy of that number: your browser asked the Treasury for it a moment ago, and will ask again every hour.

$4.2 trillion

Counting every day. The Treasury's daily series begins on April 1, 1993, when the total stood at $4.23 trillion.

It grew slowly for most of the decade and passed $5 trillion on Feb. 23, 1996.

−$90 billion

The one year it fell. The federal budget ran a surplus from 1998 to 2001. The total still rose in most of those years, because the Social Security trust funds lent their own surpluses to the Treasury.

Only in 2000 did it end the year lower than it began, by $89.5 billion. It has not happened since.

$10 trillion

The crisis. On Sept. 30, 2008, two weeks after Lehman Brothers failed, the total passed $10 trillion.

By the end of 2009 it was $12.3 trillion, and it passed $15 trillion on Nov. 15, 2011.

+$4.6 trillion

The pandemic. In 2020, as Congress paid for relief from the coronavirus, the total rose from $23.2 trillion to $27.7 trillion, the largest increase of any year in the series.

It passed $25 trillion on May 5 of that year.

$40 trillion

Forty. The total passed $30 trillion on Jan. 31, 2022, $35 trillion on July 26, 2024 and $40 trillion on Aug. 18, 2026.

The first $10 trillion of the series took until 2008. The last $15 trillion took a little over six years.

Source: U.S. Treasury, Bureau of the Fiscal Service, Debt to the Penny. Total public debt outstanding on the last business day of each month.

Who is owed

Most of it is owed to lenders outside the federal government: people and companies, pension and mutual funds, banks, state and local governments, foreign governments and the Federal Reserve. That share, the debt held by the public, was 81 percent on Sept. 24, 2026, up from 56 percent at the start of 2008.

The rest the government owes itself: securities held in its own accounts, most of them the trust funds of Social Security, federal workers' pensions and Medicare.

The debt is not the deficit. The deficit is what the government borrows in a single year, when it spends more than it takes in; the debt is everything it has borrowed and not yet paid back.

About the data

Debt to the Penny is published by the Bureau of the Fiscal Service, part of the U.S. Treasury, every business day through its Fiscal Data API (api.fiscaldata.treasury.gov), which needs no key. The first chart is fetched from it live: when this page opens, your browser asks the API for the 90 most recent business days, and asks again every hour. Until the API answers, or if it can't be reached, the chart shows the figures as they were when this page was published.

The history is the month-end total from the same series, downloaded Sept. 28, 2026, through the figure for Sept. 24. The files and the steps from them to the charts are in the article's data/ folder and SOURCES.md. Works of the U.S. government are in the public domain.
