Climate

A warming world, in four records

The planet's temperature, the gas behind it, the ice it is melting and who is putting that gas into the air. Each is a public record anyone can check, and none needs a model to tell the story.

By the datars graphics deskSept. 28, 2026

Scientists have measured the thermometer, the air and the ice for long enough now that the story is in the records themselves.

The thermometer

For the first half of the last century, the global average barely moved. Since the 1970s it has climbed almost every decade. Every year since 1977 has been warmer than the average of 1951 to 1980, and the 11 warmest years on record are the last 11.

2024 was the warmest year in the record, 1.29 degrees Celsius above the mid-century average; 2025 was the second warmest. Month by month the pattern is the same: in every season, the recent years sit highest.

The air

The cause is no mystery. Carbon dioxide traps heat, and its concentration in the atmosphere, measured continuously since 1958 on Mauna Loa in Hawaii, has risen every single year. It grew by 58 parts per million in the 25 years after 2000, more than in the 30 years before.

The ice

The Arctic shows it fastest. Each September the sea ice shrinks to its smallest, and satellites have measured it every year since 1979. The last ten Septembers averaged 4.6 million square kilometers of ice, more than a third less than the first ten. In 2012 it fell to 3.57 million, about half the 1979 extent.

The emitters

Where does the carbon come from? It depends on how you count. Total emissions point to China, which burned more fossil carbon in 2022 than the next three countries combined. Emissions per person point to the Gulf states, Australia and North America.

11.4 billion

In total, China emitted 11.4 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide from fossil fuels and cement in 2022, more than the United States, India and Russia together. Circle area is the country's emissions.

Per person, the picture flips. The average Qatari, Saudi, Australian or American emits several times as much as the average person in India, at about 2 tonnes.

About the data

Temperatures are NASA's GISTEMP v4 annual and monthly global means, as differences from the 1951–1980 average, downloaded September 2026. Carbon dioxide is NOAA's annual mean at Mauna Loa (co2_annmean_mlo.csv). Sea ice is NSIDC's Sea Ice Index, version 4: the average extent in September of each year (N_09_extent_v4.0.csv); both files are next to their charts. Emissions are the Global Carbon Project's 2023 fossil CO₂ figures for 2022, rounded. Base maps © Natural Earth.
