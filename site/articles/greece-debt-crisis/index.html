Europe

Greece's lost decade, and the debt it left behind

Three bailouts, a quarter of the economy gone and a debt that is still the heaviest in the European Union. How the crisis unfolded, and how far the recovery has come.

By the datars graphics deskSept. 28, 2026

In the autumn of 2009, a newly elected Greek government revealed that its budget deficit was more than twice what had been reported. Within months, markets stopped lending to Athens at any price it could afford, and in May 2010 Greece took the first of three international bailouts.

What followed was the deepest peacetime slump of any advanced economy in modern times.

A debt that kept growing

The cuts that came with the bailouts were meant to shrink the debt. For years they did the opposite: as the economy contracted, the debt grew as a share of it.

105%

Before the crisis, Greece's government debt was already larger than its whole economy: 105 percent of gross domestic product in 2007, against 66 percent across the euro area. By 2010 it had reached 148 percent, and Europe and the International Monetary Fund stepped in.

Three bailouts followed, in 2010, 2012 and 2015. In 2012, private bondholders were forced to accept losses on most of what they were owed, the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history, and the ratio dipped. It did not last.

209%

The long view. The ratio passed 200 percent when the pandemic hit in 2020. Since then a growing economy and higher prices have pulled it down fast, to 146 percent in 2025.

Source: Eurostat, general government gross debt.

What markets charged

For most of the euro's first decade, investors treated Greek and German debt as nearly interchangeable. In 2010 that belief broke. The interest rate Athens had to offer climbed into double digits, and by 2012 its bonds traded at prices that assumed a default, which, for private creditors, is what came.

Three rescues

Shut out of the markets, Greece borrowed from its euro area partners and the I.M.F. instead: about 289 billion euros disbursed over three programs, each with conditions attached, from spending cuts and tax increases to privatizations and pension reform. The last program ended in August 2018.

The human cost

The numbers on the balance sheet hid a social collapse. The economy that remained was much smaller: after inflation, more than a quarter of Greek output disappeared between 2008 and 2013. At the worst, in 2013, more than a quarter of the workforce had no job. Among the young, it was closer to three in five.

A quarter of the economy vanished in five years. The debt it left behind is still the heaviest in Europe.

Still at the top of the table

Greece's recovery has been real. It has grown faster than the euro area since 2021, unemployment is below 9 percent, and in 2023 it won back an investment-grade credit rating. But measured against the size of its economy, no country in the European Union owes more.

Government debt across the European Union in 2025, as a share of each economy. The heaviest burdens are in the south and west.

Ranked, Greece still tops the table, ahead of Italy, France and Belgium.

146%

Greece owes nearly a year and a half of everything its economy produces. Bringing that down will take many more years of budget surpluses.

Source: Eurostat, general government gross debt, 2025.

About the data

All series are Eurostat's, downloaded in September 2026: general government gross debt as a percentage of GDP (gov_10dd_edpt1), 10-year government bond yields used for the Maastricht convergence criterion (irt_lt_mcby_a), GDP in chain-linked volumes (nama_10_gdp, indexed here to 2008) and the unemployment rate (une_rt_a, available from 2009 in its current form). The euro area is its 20 current members throughout. The bailout total is the European Stability Mechanism's and the I.M.F.'s disbursements. Base maps © Natural Earth.
