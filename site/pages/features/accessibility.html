A chart a screen reader can read, not a picture it can't

Every mark in datars carries semantics — a role, a label, a value, a reading order — and the standard recipes fill them in: “Moderates: 68 seats”, not “rect”. Each platform's runtime hands them to its assistive technology. Below is one chart, and what each kind of reader gets from it: the list a screen reader walks, the items a keyboard reaches, the chart at twice the text size, the colours as other eyes see them, more contrast and forced colours, and less motion.

    Colour vision

    A simulation over the drawn chart — the same matrices the engine's palette checks use. These are the parties' own colours, set by the document: under deuteranopia the Social Democrats' red and the Centre's green move closer. Every bar is named on its axis and carries its value, so nothing hangs on colour alone.

    Contrast

    A browser can't recolour a canvas, so under forced colours the element paints the chart's text, rules and surfaces in the system's own colours itself — CanvasText on Canvas, Highlight for the accent — and keeps the data colours that tell the parties apart.

    Your device asks for reduced motion: the chart already plays it. Untick to see the full motion.

    With reduced motion every change is a short crossfade instead of bars growing and receding, and autoplay and clocks stay still. It's the reader's system setting by default — prefers-reduced-motion, iOS Reduce Motion, Android's Remove animations — and a page can decide per chart:

    On every platform

    The native way, everywhere

    • Web

      A figure named by the chart, its summary — what it shows, how many points, their range — as its description. Beside the canvas, kept in step with it: the data as tables (name and value columns), axes and legends as a line each, clickable marks as buttons, engine-drawn sliders and dropdowns as native controls, real links over linked text. One Tab stop into the data, arrow keys through it, each mark outlined and named on the chart; a brush or a pannable view one stop with keys of its own; a polite live region announces each step once, and what a click or a key changed. Checked in the accessibility trees of Chromium, WebKit and Gecko.

    • iOS and macOS

      VoiceOver walks the chart first (a summary element), its controls (sliders say their value as drawn), headings for its parts, then each mark with its name, value and frame, so explore-by-touch finds each bar where it is drawn; activatable marks, a brush adjustable; step announcements and what an action changed. Increase Contrast and Bold Text followed, live. Dumped and operated in the simulator; not yet driven by VoiceOver itself.

    • Android

      TalkBack reads the same order through an accessibility node provider: virtual nodes with screen bounds, headings, values, clickable marks, adjustable sliders, a brush adjustable, the story's steps as buttons, step announcements and what an action changed; the reader's contrast and bold-text settings followed, live. Driven with TalkBack itself on an emulator — real swipes and taps, what it spoke recorded in the accessibility guide.

    • Desktop

      The same through AccessKit, for VoiceOver, Narrator/NVDA and Orca: a figure with its summary, interactive marks as buttons, sliders adjustable — unit-tested, not yet exercised with a real screen reader.

    • Video

      WebVTT captions generated from the story's narration, alongside every MP4 datars video renders.

    • SVG and PDF

      SVG exports and posters carry titles, descriptions and roles; PDF keeps text as searchable text.

    For every reader

    Beyond screen readers

    Intents, not mouse events

    Documents bind inspect and activate, not hover and click: activate is a click, a tap, Enter on the mark's button, or a double-tap with VoiceOver or TalkBack. Every input, intent by intent →

    Touch that doesn't trap

    On a phone a finger that lands on a brush, a pannable map or a slider drags it; anywhere else it scrolls the page, so a chart the width of the screen never holds the reader.

    Bigger text, laid out for it

    The browser's font size, Dynamic Type or Android's font size grows the chart's labels — and the chart lays out again around them: axes widen, ticks thin, names wrap, labels that would collide give way. Live, as the setting changes; text-scale when the page knows better.

    Contrast the reader asks for

    More contrast is the theme's high-contrast mode; a Windows contrast theme paints the chart's text, rules and surfaces in the system's own colours.

    Reduced motion, per chart

    The system setting by default; reduced-motion="reduce" or "no-preference" when the page knows better — an opt-in “show me the motion” button, a side-by-side comparison.

    Colour you can tell apart

    Themes are checked for WCAG contrast of text on its background, and generated palettes for colours that stay distinct under simulated protanopia, deuteranopia and tritanopia. The theme studio shows the checks →

    Text that is text

    Titles, labels and annotations lie over the canvas as real text: selectable, findable with Ctrl+F, enlarged with the page.

    Data, not just a picture

    Every published bundle carries an SVG poster with titles and roles, and its accessible text — the chart is understandable before the runtime loads, and where it never does.

    Legibility

    Every chart, read on a phone with big text, before anyone has to

    datars labels lays every state of a chart out at a phone's width, a tablet's and a wide column's, each at the theme's text size and at 1.5 and 2 ×, and counts the labels a reader couldn't read there: cut off at the edge, drawn over one another, clipped by their box, or sitting on the data with a line through them. The repository runs it over its examples in datars test, where a chart that gets harder to read fails. Here it is over the example charts, at this build:

    {{run:labels examples --widths 390,768 --text-scale 1,1.5,2|40}}

    What's left is at 2 × on a phone — about the room of a 195 px chart at the theme's size. What it found and fixed so far — axis labels drawn across bars when names turned, legends and controls that didn't grow with the text, tables wider than the screen, labels on the lines they label — is in the guide.

    Caught before publishing

    The linter knows what makes a chart inaccessible

    datars lint checks identity, encoding, legibility, accessibility and the theme: interactive marks without an accessible label, labels that collide or are cut off at the edge, more categorical colours than readers can tell apart, text colours that fail the theme's WCAG contrast checks, palettes that collapse under colour-vision deficiencies, rows without keys, and maps without the credit their data's licence requires. Agents get the same findings over MCP.

    Plainly

    What isn't there yet

    • One screen reader driven so far: TalkBack, on an emulator, with what it said recorded. The web element's tree is checked in Chromium, WebKit and Gecko and read through the macOS Accessibility API (what VoiceOver gets); the iOS elements are dumped and operated in the simulator. VoiceOver, NVDA and JAWS haven't been driven, and real devices and the Safari browser itself are untested.
    • A click's result is said generically: the clicked mark's new label, the parts whose label changed, how much data is shown now. A selection that only changes how marks look is not announced unless its label says it.
    • Safari's Tab key skips buttons unless the reader asks it not to: there, Tab goes from the chart to its data; the chart's own arrow keys still step the story.
    • Long lists stop at 500 marks on the web — a map of thousands of counties is in the text description and the summary's count, not read out mark by mark.
    • Text size follows the theme's sizes only. Text a document sizes in pixels stays as written; posters and videos are at the theme's size; iOS and Android apps get the height bigger text needs (contentHeight) but lay their views out themselves.
    • Two languages. The words the element adds — the summary, “Step 2 of 4”, captions — are English and Swedish so far; other locales read them in English.
