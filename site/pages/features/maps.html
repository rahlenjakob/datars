Maps, no tile server

From the globe to a street corner — no tile server, no API key, no bill

In datars a basemap is a node in the scene, a country is a keyed shape like a bar, and one camera flies from space to a street. The streets come from one static file on your own host, cut when you publish for exactly the places your document visits. The map below is that: pick a place and fly there, turn layers on and off, restyle the basemap — and watch what the page actually downloads.

Automatic basemaps

The document says where it goes; the build cuts the map

The playground’s document asks for data.tiles.auto() and has one state per place. At publish time the build evaluates every state’s camera, and 48 frames of each flight between them, works out which tiles each view needs at which zoom, fetches the data for exactly those, and writes one archive. This is that plan, printed by the build of this page:

  • Open data, fetched once. Natural Earth for the world, OpenStreetMap from zoom 10: coastline-built land, water, rivers, parks, roads by class, buildings from zoom 13, place names.
  • A cache and a lockfile. Downloads go into a local geodata cache, so rebuilds are offline and byte-identical; the archive’s job file records what it was made from.
  • Polite by default. Public OpenStreetMap servers are used one request at a time within a daily budget; teams point the build at their own instance or a static cell server with one environment variable.
  • datars dev never waits: the preview shows Natural Earth at once and swaps streets in as they arrive.

Maps are scenes

Projections, layers and globes — and everything keyed

Regions, symbols and dots carry the keys of their rows, so a change of projection, layer or chart type is a transition the engine plans, not a redraw. Each of these is a small document; step through them.

Projections

One atlas, five projections

Each state is the same map in another projection. The countries are keyed by ISO code, so each one morphs from where it is to where the next projection puts it — even onto the far side of a globe.

map({ source: "world", data: "urban", key: "id", value: "share",
  projection: "orthographic", fit: { sphere: true } })

Layers · keyed

One table, four maps

A choropleth, proportional symbols, dot density and bars from the same rows. The dots are keyed by country and dot number, so each circle breaks into its own dots; the countries then lift off the map into their bars.

Clocks · great circles

A globe that turns by itself

The projection’s centre is an expression over a clock signal, so countries, routes and hubs turn together. Routes are great circles, cut at the horizon. The clock stops off screen and for readers who asked for less motion.

signals: { spin: signal.clock(6) },   // 6° a second
coord: { type: "geo", projection: "orthographic",

What’s in the box

Everything a map story needs

Choropleths as a join

The built-in countries atlas (ISO 3166-1 alpha-3 ids) ships as a compact topology, shared by hash across every chart that uses it. map colours regions from your table; bring your own regions as GeoJSON or TopoJSON.

Symbols, dots, routes, tracks

symbols at each region’s visual centre, dotDensity scattered evenly inside regions, route along great circles, track drawn up to a moment in time, points and lines at longitude and latitude.

Projections a story needs

Web Mercator, Equal Earth, Natural Earth, Albers and Albers USA, Lambert conformal conic, an orthographic globe, transverse Mercator and national grids — plus planar coordinates for floor plans and fantasy worlds.

Your theme on the map

Water, land, roads, parks, buildings, borders and labels are theme tokens (map.water, map.land, …), so the basemap follows your brand and the reader’s dark mode. The basemap is a recipe you can eject.

Labels that don’t fight

Place names and your own labels go through the same placement engine, by priority, without overlaps; names draw above your data layer.

Credits handled

OpenStreetMap’s licence requires attribution. attribution draws it, linked; datars lint flags a map that would ship without it.

What it saves you

No keys, no quotas, no per-view pricing

A basemap is one file next to your chart, on your host or CDN, read in ranges as the camera needs them — the playground’s is {{archive:maps-playground}} for seventeen places from the world to two street corners, and a reader downloads only the parts they fly over. There’s nothing to sign up for and nothing metered, it keeps working when a vendor changes its terms, and because it’s the same engine, the flight in your iOS app, on your site and in the exported video is the same flight, frame for frame.

Limits, plainly. An automatic basemap covers the views its document visits and the flights between them (and extra zoom levels around maps a reader can pan and zoom, within a tile budget) — not the whole planet at street level; elsewhere the map shows the nearest coarser tiles, enlarged. Building one fetches OpenStreetMap data, once per place, from public servers that are shared and sometimes slow; a team making many maps should use its own Overpass instance or pre-cut cells.
